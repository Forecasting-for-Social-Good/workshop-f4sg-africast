Introduction to forecasting

What is a forecast?

Estimating how a sequence of observations will continue into the future, using all information available now.

What data do we need?

  1. Historical data on the variable to forecast
  2. Past and future values of predictors
    • Deterministic (e.g. holidays)
    • Stochastic (e.g. temperature)
  3. Expert knowledge and context
    • New information

Why forecast?

Why do you use forecasts?

Decision Forecast
Build a new hospital in the next 10 years? Population and patient demand, years ahead
Call centre staff needed next week? Call volumes, hours to days ahead
Vaccine doses needed next month? Doses by region, weeks ahead

The decision sets what to forecast and how far ahead.

What time series data do you work with?

What makes it a time series?

Observations + when they happened.

  • The time column is the index
  • Observations are ordered in time
  • Regular intervals define the frequency (yearly, quarterly, …)

tsibble objects

A tsibble has three parts

A tsibble stores one or more time series in a table.

  • Index: when
  • Key: which series (optional)
  • Measures: what was observed

Works with dplyr verbs (filter(), mutate(), …).

Converting dates

Often data is provided as dates, even if it isn’t daily.

my_date <- as.Date("2026-10-01")
yearquarter(my_date)
<yearquarter[1]>
[1] "2026 Q4"
# Year starts on: January
yearmonth(my_date)
<yearmonth[1]>
[1] "2026 Oct"
yearweek(my_date)
<yearweek[1]>
[1] "2026 W40"
# Week starts on: Monday

Looking ahead: mixtime

One family of linear time classes, including year() and datetime():

library(mixtime)
c(year(my_date), yearquarter(my_date), yearmonth(my_date), date(my_date))
<mixtime[4]>
[1] 2026       2026 Q4    2026 Oct   2026-10-01

Any granularity: pick a chronon (one time step) and call linear_time():

quarter_hours <- datetime("2024-01-01 00:00:00") + minutes(0:3) * 15L
linear_time(quarter_hours, chronon = cal_gregorian$minute(15L))
<mixtime[4]>
[1] 2024-01-01 00:00 2024-01-01 00:15 2024-01-01 00:30 2024-01-01 00:45
  • yearmonth(my_date) is linear_time(my_date, chronon = cal_gregorian$month(1L))

What a tsibble checks

  • Index + key must uniquely identify each row: as_tsibble() errors on duplicates (find them with duplicates())
  • The interval is shown in the header: [1Y], [1Q], [1M], …
  • Missing time steps are implicit gaps: find them with has_gaps(), make them explicit with fill_gaps()
tsibble(year = c(2012, 2013, 2015, 2016), y = c(123, 39, 52, 110), index = year) |>
  fill_gaps()
# A tsibble: 5 x 2 [1Y]
   year     y
  <dbl> <dbl>
1  2012   123
2  2013    39
3  2014    NA
4  2015    52
5  2016   110

Example: Australian pharmaceutical sales

Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme

Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme

The PBS is the Australian government drug subsidy scheme.

  • Subsidises many pharmacy drugs
  • Cost the government A$19.1 billion in 2024–25 (about 0.7% of GDP)
  • Budget relies on forecasts of drug usage

PBS data: monthly, July 1991 to June 2008

  • Scripts (prescriptions) and Cost (A$)
  • By drug type (ATC2 x84), concession (x2) and patient type (x2)
  • ATC: Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification

Recap

  • A tsibble has an index, keys and measures
  • Index + keys uniquely identify each row
  • The index needs a time class (yearmonth(), as_date(), …)
  • Use dplyr verbs to wrangle tsibbles
  • summarise() combines over keys, keeping the index
